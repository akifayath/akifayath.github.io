About

I am a river scientist interested in how rivers and deltas evolve in engineering timescales, particularly when bifurcations are present. My Ph.D. at TU Delft looked at the Upper Rhine delta, where the river bifurcates into several branches. Combining a century of measurements with a one-dimensional hydro-morphological model, I examined how flow and sediment are shared between those branches, and how that may change under future climate.

Before this, I studied three-dimensional flow structure in the Wax Lake Delta (Louisiana) during my M.Sc. at Louisiana State University. My first degree was in naval architecture and marine engineering at Bangladesh University of Engineering and Technology, Dhaka.

Research

One question runs through my work: when a river splits in two, where does the water go, and what changes that over time? Here is the story in four steps.

Step 1 · How a split works

Where does the water go when a river splits?

What happens at the water column at a channel bifurcation is essential for flow and sediment partitioning.

Water and sediment spilling sideways from a delta distributary channel are major phenomena influencing the connectivity and morphological development of a delta. In the Wax Lake Delta in Louisiana, I measured various flow attributes from a boat and investigated how water leaving a channel affects the three-dimensional flow structure at the channel-island interface and, in turn, where sediment ultimately ends up.

Step 2 · A river held in place

What did centuries of engineering and peak flows do to the bifurcating Lower Rhine reach?

Engineering interventions are not the only factors dominating the hydro-morphological development of the Lower Rhine River system.

The Rhine splits twice in the Netherlands, and its planform, banks and bed have been engineered for centuries. Using hydraulic and morphological measurements over the last century, I showed how floods and human interventions gradually changed the distribution of water over time. Since the 1990s, we have observed a trend change triggered by a sequence of peak flows, resulting in a consistently increasing proportion of the Rhine’s water flowing into one branch (the Waal).

Step 3 · A warning sign?

Is the river heading for a tipping point?

Big floods can nudge a bifurcating river into a new equilibrium, and the Rhine shows signs that this may have happened.

With Astrid Blom and colleagues, I examined long river records for early signs that random noise, such as large floods, can push a system past a threshold and change its trajectory toward a new equilibrium configuration.

Step 4 · Looking ahead

How does climate change impact bifurcating river systems?

From around 2050, climate-related changes may gradually become a stronger force on the bifurcating Rhine River system, shaping its development and influencing management.

I simulated 150 years of the Rhine delta development (hydraulic and morphological) under different climate scenarios. As sea level rises, it starts to matter more for how the water divides at the bifurcations at the end of the century, while changes in river flow mainly erode the riverbed further. This affects flood safety, freshwater supply, and shipping.

Teaching Philosophy

I see teaching as a mutual learning process, not just a delivery of facts. I rely on everyday examples and analogies to make complex river processes feel intuitive, then build up to the details so students feel connected to a topic. This connection motivates students to learn, and in my experience students with different backgrounds benefit from this approach. My aim is for students to leave able to ask their own good questions, and to feel confident doing so.
